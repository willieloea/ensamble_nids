\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern communication networks face a continuous and evolving spectrum of cyberattacks, ranging from high-volume denial-of-service floods and automated reconnaissance scans to low-footprint shellcode injections, backdoors, and self-propagating worms. Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) play a critical role in defending these infrastructures by inspecting flow-level packet, protocol, and content features to distinguish benign traffic from specific attack categories~\cite{Moustafa2015}. Framing multi-category intrusion detection as a supervised classification problem introduces several practical challenges: network traffic combines high-cardinality categorical protocol descriptors with skewed continuous rate and timing counters, exhibits strong collinearity among flow statistics, and suffers from severe class imbalance in which benign flows and common attacks outnumber rare exploits by orders of magnitude.

Decision trees are an appealing baseline for intrusion detection because they naturally accommodate mixed categorical and numerical predictors without requiring feature rescaling or one-hot encoding, and they express predictions as explicit, human-interpretable rule paths~\cite{Kelleher2020,Quinlan1986}. However, unrestricted decision trees grown greedily until leaf homogeneity are high-variance learners that readily memorise noisy or conflicting flow records. Two distinct paradigms exist to address this limitation: structural regularisation of a single tree (through depth constraints or post-pruning on held-out observations) and ensemble learning, which combines multiple base learners via bootstrap aggregation (bagging) or sequential adaptive reweighting (boosting)~\cite{Breiman1996,Freund1997,Zhu2009}.

The primary objective of this study is to answer the central comparative question: \emph{How does the predictive performance of a standalone decision tree compare with ensembles of decision trees for multiclass network intrusion detection on the UNSW-NB15 dataset?} Rather than comparing an ensemble against a dissimilar model family (such as $k$-nearest neighbours or linear classifiers), we intentionally hold the base learner family fixed across all experiments. Every single-model and ensemble configuration is built around the same custom Iterative Dichotomiser 3 (ID3) decision-tree implementation extended for continuous thresholds and reduced-error pruning. This controlled choice ensures that observed differences in classification performance, class-level recall, and computational cost are attributable to the aggregation or regularisation mechanism itself rather than differences in feature representation or inductive bias.

To investigate this question systematically, we partition the 257,673-row dataset into fixed, stratified Growth (123,683 rows), Pruning (41,225 rows), Validation (41,234 rows), and reserved Generalization (51,531 rows) sets, and address four guiding sub-questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How does model performance scale as the training set grows, and how strongly does natural class scarcity in small samples constrain minority-attack detection relative to ensemble aggregation?
    \item How do tree depth limits, reduced-error post-pruning, and class-balanced resampling interact to control overfitting and trade off overall accuracy against minority-class recall?
    \item How do bagging and multiclass SAMME boosting behave as ensemble size, base-learner depth, and learning rate vary?
    \item In a final comparison making full use of the dataset under a shared hybrid class-balancing policy, how do an unpruned tree, a reduced-error-pruned tree, bagging, and SAMME boosting compare on the untouched generalization set?
\end{enumerate}

Our empirical findings challenge the default assumption that ensemble methods automatically outperform a single decision tree when macro-F1 across all ten traffic classes is the primary objective. In small-sample screening (500 to 2,000 rows) and learning-curve experiments (up to 50,000 rows), increasing the volume of original training data produced much larger macro-F1 gains than ensembling alone, while unweighted accuracy-based pruning on small natural samples hurt macro-F1 by collapsing sparse minority branches. However, in the final comparison making full use of the 123,683-row growth pool under a hybrid class-balancing policy and pruning against all 41,225 dedicated pruning rows, this interaction reversed: balancing the training distribution allowed minority-attack subtrees to grow, while reduced-error pruning removed overfitted high-false-positive leaves. On the reserved generalization set, the pruned single tree achieved the highest mean macro-F1 ($0.5993$), highest balanced accuracy ($63.02\%$), and highest overall accuracy ($80.73\%$), outperforming 25-member bagging ($0.5747$ macro-F1), the unpruned tree ($0.5545$), and 25-round depth-8 SAMME boosting ($0.5408$). A supplementary validation extension further confirmed diminishing returns when expanding bagging from 25 to 50 members.
