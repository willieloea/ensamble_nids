\begin{abstract}
Network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) must classify heterogeneous network flows across severely imbalanced traffic categories, where high overall accuracy can conceal poor recognition of rare cyberattacks. This study investigates how a standalone decision tree compares with decision-tree ensemble methods on a 10-class subset of the UNSW-NB15 dataset comprising 257,673 cleaned observations and 41 predictors. By fixing an extended Iterative Dichotomiser 3 (ID3) learner as the common base classifier, the experimental design isolates the effects of post-pruning, bootstrap aggregation (bagging), and multiclass AdaBoost (SAMME) from differences between unrelated model families.

Using a fixed stratified four-way split that separates tree growth (123,683 rows), reduced-error pruning (41,225 rows), validation (41,234 rows), and final generalization testing (51,531 rows), we follow a progressive diagnostic methodology. Preliminary small-sample screening, a correlation ablation, and 50,000-row learning-curve studies show that training sample size and minority class support strongly govern predictive performance, and that removing strongly correlated predictors degrades macro-F1.

In a final comparison making full use of the dataset and a hybrid class-balancing policy, a single reduced-error-pruned decision tree achieved the strongest generalization performance with a mean macro-F1 of $0.5993$, balanced accuracy of $63.02\%$, and overall accuracy of $80.73\%$. Twenty-five-member bagging improved reliably over the unpruned tree ($0.5747$ vs.\ $0.5545$ macro-F1), whereas depth-8 SAMME boosting lagged ($0.5408$). A supplementary validation extension further confirmed diminishing returns when expanding bagging from 25 to 50 members. These findings demonstrate that when training classes are partially balanced and a dedicated pruning set is available, explicit structural regularisation of a single tree can outperform substantially more expensive ensembles under the tested configurations.
\end{abstract}
